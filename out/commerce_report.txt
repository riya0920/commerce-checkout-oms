1. FLASH SALE -- 1000 CONCURRENT CHECKOUTS, 50 UNITS
==============================================================================
mechanism    attempted   placed   sold_out oversell    errors     tput/s   p95 ms   p99 ms
optimistic        1000       50        950        0         0      365.9    96.53   885.12
pessimistic       1000       50        950        0         0      419.9    48.84   780.15

optimistic   stock on_hand=0 reserved=0 committed_reservations=50
             invariant violations: none
pessimistic  stock on_hand=0 reserved=0 committed_reservations=50
             invariant violations: none

Exactly 50 succeed and the rest get a clean sold-out. No oversell is
possible because the check and the decrement are ONE statement: the
optimistic UPDATE carries `AND version = ? AND on_hand - reserved >= ?`
in its WHERE clause, so a loser changes nothing and sees rowcount 0.

READ THE THROUGHPUT COLUMN WITH THE SUBSTITUTION IN MIND. SQLite
serialises writers at the database level, so this is NOT a measurement
of optimistic vs pessimistic row-level concurrency -- both mechanisms
are queueing behind the same global write lock. What the comparison
does show is the cost of the optimistic RETRY path under contention:
every loser re-reads and re-issues, and on a 50-unit sale with 1,000
shoppers almost everyone is a loser. On Postgres with row locks the
pessimistic column would be the one paying, and the honest version of
this benchmark needs that engine. Claiming otherwise from this data
would be the exact overreach the spec is screening for.

2. PSP TIMED OUT AFTER CAPTURE -- THE UGLY ONE
==============================================================================
60 checkouts against a PSP timing out 35% of the time after capture:
  placed             35
  capture_unknown    25   <- money may have moved, nobody knows

Reconciliation job asks the PSP what actually happened:
  examined 25   confirmed 25   voided 0
  orders left in capture_unknown: 0
  capture movements before/after: 35 -> 60

Re-running the job (it is on a schedule, so it WILL run again):
  examined 0   confirmed 0   voided 0  <- no-op, as required
  orders with more than one capture movement: 0

PSP-side check -- did we charge anyone twice?
  captures settled at the PSP: 60
  capture movements in our ledger: 60

The customer is charged once or not at all, and a paid order is never
silently dropped. The mechanism is that the idempotency key we send
the PSP is derived from our own attempt id, so the reconciliation job
can SEARCH for it afterwards. A capture sent without a key you can
look up later makes this ambiguity permanently unresolvable.
  ledger invariant violations: none

3. THE EXPIRY SWEEPER CRASHES MID-BATCH
==============================================================================
before sweep:  held=200  on_hand=500  reserved=200
crashed the sweeper 5 times mid-batch, then ran it to completion.
after:         held=0  released=200  on_hand=500  reserved=0
  invariant violations: none

WHAT IS GUARANTEED ON RESTART: every reservation is released exactly
once. The guarantee comes from the state guard, not from bookkeeping --
the UPDATE that frees the stock is predicated on the row still being
'held' and lives in the same transaction that marks it 'released', so a
crash leaves a released PREFIX and untouched remainder. There is no
interleaving in which stock is returned twice or leaks permanently.

4. DOUBLE-SUBMIT
==============================================================================
24 concurrent submits of one idempotency key:
  distinct order ids returned to callers: 1
  orders in the database:                 1
  capture movements:                      1
  stock consumed:                         1

One order, one capture, one unit. The arbiter is a UNIQUE constraint on
idempotency_key -- not a check-then-insert, which loses the very race it
was written to prevent. The loser of the INSERT catches IntegrityError
and reads back the winner's order, so every caller gets the same answer.

5. PARTIAL RETURN OF A DISCOUNTED ORDER -- THE ARITHMETIC
==============================================================================
Order: 3 items, $90.00 gross, 20% off order = -$18.00
  order-level discount allocated to lines: $6.00, $3.00, $9.00
  tax (8.75%) = $6.30, grand total = $78.30

Shipped in two parcels (DC1: lines 0,1 / DC2: line 2), delivered.

Customer returns 1 of 3 items -- the PAN:
  gross price                 $   45.00
  less its share of the 20%    -$    9.00
  plus its share of the tax   +$    3.15
  ------------------------------------
  REFUND                      $   39.15

The customer never paid $45.00 for that pan -- they paid $36.00 plus
tax, because the order discount was theirs proportionally. Refunding
the ticket price would hand back $9.00 of discount that was never
charged. WHO DEFINED THE POLICY: proportional allocation is set at
checkout by the promotions engine (se2), not invented at return time --
which is why se2's allocation invariant is exact to the cent.

Ledger after the partial refund:
  captured  $   78.30
  refunded  $   39.15
  net       $   39.15   refunds <= captured: True

Then the customer returns the other two:
  captured  $   78.30
  refunded  $   78.30
  residual  $    0.00  (this is shipping+rounding, not a leak)

Returning every unit one return-event at a time refunds EXACTLY the
captured amount. Per-unit discount and tax shares are apportioned by
largest remainder, so no cent is created on the first return or
stranded on the last -- which is the failure a naive per-unit division
produces and which surfaces months later as an unexplainable penny.

Audit trail (the CS-agent view):
  -                      -> pending                checkout started
  pending                -> reserved               {"reservations": 3}
  reserved               -> placed                 captured 7830
  placed                 -> allocated              
  allocated              -> picked                 
  picked                 -> packed                 
  packed                 -> partially_shipped      warehouse=DC1 {0: 1, 1: 1}
  partially_shipped      -> shipped                warehouse=DC2 {2: 1}
  shipped                -> delivered              
  delivered              -> return_requested       
  return_requested       -> partially_returned     refund=3915
  partially_returned     -> returned               refund=3915
  MONEY capture      7830  checkout
  MONEY refund       3915  return
  MONEY refund       3915  return

ledger invariant violations: none

6. ALLOCATION -- WHICH WAREHOUSE SHIPS WHICH LINE
==============================================================================
The first pass took `warehouse` as a STRING ARGUMENT to ship(). The
split across DCs in drill 5 was a test fixture describing a split, not
a system computing one. This computes it.

Stock position (units free):
  DC          TEE    MUG    PAN    dist km
  DC_NJ        10      0     10         14
  DC_OH        10     10      0        765
  DC_NV        50     50     50       3585

Order: TEE x2, MUG x1, PAN x1, shipping to New York.
No single DC can fill it: NJ has no MUG, OH has no PAN.

Allocator chose 2 parcel(s), cost $46.26:
  DC_NJ    -> TEE x2, PAN x1
  DC_OH    -> MUG x1

The rule everyone writes first -- 'nearest DC that has everything,
else split' -- finds that only DC_NV can fill the whole order, and
ships one parcel from Nevada at $150.58.
The allocator ships TWO parcels from the east coast for $46.26 --
$104.32 cheaper, or 31% of the naive cost.

The extra parcel costs $6.50 in pick-pack-label; the 3,585 km it avoids
costs far more. That is the trade the naive rule cannot see, because
'minimise splits' and 'minimise distance' are different objectives
and it only has the first one.

The answer is a function of the cost constants, not of the code:
PARCEL_COST_CENTS = $6.50 and COST_PER_KM_CENTS = 4 are MERCHANT
INPUTS, not physics. Raise the parcel cost far enough and the single
far parcel wins. They are named constants at the top of
src/allocation.py precisely so a merchant can argue with them rather
than discovering them buried in a comparison.

WHAT THIS IS NOT: a network optimisation. It scores one order at a
time with no view of the orders behind it, no inventory-position
forecast, and no capacity reservation. Draining the near DC to save
a parcel today is a cost you pay tomorrow, and nothing here models
tomorrow.

7. EXCHANGES AS LINKED ORDERS
==============================================================================
The tempting implementation is refund-then-reorder. It fails three
ways a customer notices: the replacement is not reserved so their
size sells out mid-flight; they see a debit before the credit clears
and call support about being double-charged; and the price may have
moved so an even swap becomes an argument.

Customer bought TEE-M for $30.00 (captured $32.63 incl. tax).

  EVEN swap for TEE-L    returned $32.63, replacement $32.63 ($30.00 + $2.63 tax), NET $0.00
                         money movements for the swap: 0 (none -- even swap, no money moves)
                         child order ord_05ee26f7, stock held before the return processed
                         ledger violations: none
  UP-swap for TEE-XL     returned $32.63, replacement $39.15 ($36.00 + $3.15 tax), NET $6.52
                         money movements for the swap: 1 (capture $6.52)
                         child order ord_fe7726e8, stock held before the return processed
                         ledger violations: none

An EVEN exchange moves NO money at all. Refund-then-reorder would
have produced two movements -- a refund and a capture -- for a
transaction whose net value is zero, and the customer would have
watched their money leave and come back.

The replacement is RESERVED BEFORE the return is processed. If the
size is gone the exchange fails cleanly and the customer still has
their original item -- rather than being refunded into a stockout.
  replacement out of stock -> ok=False reason=replacement_out_of_stock
  parent order still delivered, refunds issued: 0

==============================================================================
8. REPRICING BETWEEN CART AND CHECKOUT
==============================================================================
Prices move while a cart sits open. The question is not whether they
change, it is who absorbs it and up to what threshold. Charging the
new higher price silently is the failure that produces chargebacks;
honouring a stale price forever turns a pricing error into a
promotion. Policy: absorb rises up to the SMALLER of $3.00 or 5.0%,
always pass decreases on, re-prompt above the threshold.

scenario                         quoted    current       decision    charged
unchanged                        $50.00     $50.00      unchanged     $50.00
fell to $45                      $50.00     $45.00        reduced     $45.00
rose $2 (within tol.)            $50.00     $52.00       honoured     $50.00
rose $12 (over tol.)             $50.00     $62.00      reconfirm          -

`reconfirm` is a REAL outcome, not an error path. The alternative --
charging more than the customer agreed to -- costs a chargeback, and
a chargeback costs more than the abandoned cart does. Every decision
is written to order_events, so a CS agent asked 'why did this cost
more than the email said' has an answer.

9. OPS METRICS -- WHAT THE EVENT LOG WAS ALWAYS FOR
==============================================================================
order_events existed from the first commit and nothing read it except
the CS-agent audit view. This is the other consumer, and it is the
reason to write transitions to a LOG rather than only mutating a
state column: the funnel is a QUERY over history, not a set of
counters someone remembered to increment.

300 checkouts against a PSP declining 8% and timing out 10%,
against 220 units of stock:

step           orders     % of started    step conversion
started           300           100.0%             100.0%
reserved          144            48.0%              48.0%
placed            116            38.7%              80.6%
shipped            99            33.0%              85.3%
delivered          91            30.3%              91.9%

On-call health:
  reservation_expiry_rate    0.056
  payment_failure_rate       0.027
  out_of_stock_rate          0.520
  capture_unknown_open       20
  reservations_held_now      20

capture_unknown_open is the one to page on. It is not a rate, it is
a COUNT of orders where money may have moved and nobody knows --
every one of them is a customer who may have been charged for an
order that does not exist. It should be driven to zero by the
reconciliation job in section 2, and if it is not, the job is broken.

The out_of_stock_rate is high here BY CONSTRUCTION -- 300 checkouts
against 220 units -- and that is worth saying rather than presenting
it as a finding. It is a fixture, not a measurement of anything.
